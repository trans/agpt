This is a sharp writeup, Tom. The diagnosis of why dist-rope failed is correct and the harmonic-filter reframing is the right instinct. But I think the Q-position question as posed has a hidden assumption baked in, and once you pull on it the answer to "which option" changes.
Let me give you my actual opinion rather than just ranking A–E.
The core issue: you're asking Q to live in a coordinate that doesn't exist
The matched filter wants Re(Q*·K) = E_p[cos((p − q)·ω)] to spike when q = p_k. For that spike to mean anything, q has to be a sample from the same generating process as the p's in K's chord. That's the whole game.
In a standard transformer this is automatic because the query token is one of the corpus occurrences — it's literally sitting at a real position. In AGPT, Q is a node, and a node is an equivalence class over positions. So the honest statement is: there is no q. There's a distribution over q, and it's the same distribution as K's chord (when Q and K are the same node) or a related one (when they're ancestor/descendant nodes on the path).
This is why D collapses and why A is suspect: both try to substitute a single deterministic scalar for something that is irreducibly a distribution.
What this implies
If Q's position is genuinely a distribution, then the "correct" matched-filter quantity isn't a spike at one q — it's the cross-correlation of the two position distributions:
E_{p~K, q~Q}[cos((p − q)·ω)] = Re( χ_Q(ω)* · χ_K(ω) )
where χ(ω) = Σ_p (count(p)/total) e^{jpω} is the normalized chord. The attention signal at dim-pair i becomes the inner product of the two normalized chords. That's a measure of how much the two nodes' position distributions overlap in phase — i.e., do these two substrings tend to occur at the same positions mod W?
That's actually a meaningful, content-independent signal. And notice it doesn't require inventing a q at all.
But — and this is the catch that kills the naive version — if you normalize both sides you reintroduce magnitude collapse on Q exactly the way dist-rope had it on K. The thing that made the harmonic filter work was the asymmetry: sharp Q probe, un-normalized K chord. The auto-α-gate lives on the K side. The moment you make Q a chord too, you lose the unit-magnitude Q that protects sharp-K dot products.
So you have a genuine tension:

Deterministic q (A/D): coordinate mismatch, spike doesn't fire, you're gambling.
Symmetric chords (the "honest" distributional answer): clean semantics, but magnitude collapse returns and the α-gate asymmetry is gone.
Stochastic q (B/C): recovers the sharp-probe asymmetry per fire, and over many fires the expected gradient is exactly the cross-correlation above. This is the key realization — B/C are the stochastic estimator of the symmetric-chord signal, but they preserve the magnitude asymmetry on every individual fire.

That reframing is why I'd push you toward C, not as "the cleanest of four options" but because it's the only one that gets the distributional semantics without paying the magnitude-collapse tax. The variance you're worried about isn't a bug; it's the Monte Carlo cost of keeping the Q probe sharp.
On C vs B specifically
C is right over B, and your own sub-option already contains the answer. The concern you raise — "how do we sample an anchor consistent with the chunk's path" — I think you're overcomplicating it.
The chunk's path is a length-d string. Its occurrence positions are exactly the occurrence positions of the deepest node on the path (the full-depth substring), because any position where the full path occurs is a position where every prefix occurs at the corresponding offset. So:

Sample one anchor a from the deepest node's position table (mod W). That table already exists.
Query at depth k gets position (a − (d−1) + k) mod W, so the deepest query sits exactly at a and shallower queries sit at their true relative offsets behind it.

This is cleaner than "use the first query node's positions" because the first (shallowest) node is the most ambiguous — "t" occurs everywhere, so anchoring on it gives you almost no localization. The deepest node is the most specific and its position set is the actual intersection you want. You're not sampling from a "chunk-level set" you have to construct; the deepest node is the chunk-level set.
One alias table, one int per chunk, one RNG draw per fire. That's the whole cost.
On the variance concern (your subsidiary question)
This is the one place I'd genuinely temper expectations. The gradient through a stochastic rotation is unbiased for the cross-correlation objective, but the variance scales with how broad the anchor distribution is — which is worst for exactly the high-mass paths. There's a clean mitigation that keeps it deterministic-per-fire: antithetic or stratified sampling across the epoch. Since each node fires once per epoch, and you control the RNG, seed the anchor draw on (node_id, epoch) and stratify so that over E epochs a node's anchors tile its position distribution rather than redrawing iid. You get the expectation right with far lower variance, and it's reproducible. Essentially you're turning the per-fire sample into a low-discrepancy sequence over the node's positions. Cheap, and it directly addresses "does variance compromise the experiment."
Is there an option E?
The one you haven't listed and that I think is worth a thought: make K's chord encode relative-distance distribution rather than absolute-position-mod-W distribution. Right now the chord answers "where does this substring sit mod W." But what attention usually wants is "how far back." If for each node you build the chord over the distribution of gaps (distance from a query to the node's nearest prior occurrence), then Q's available coordinate — chunk-local depth, which is a relative offset within the window — lives in the same space as the chord, and the coordinate mismatch you identified largely dissolves. Depth is a bad proxy for absolute position but a fine proxy for relative offset. This is option E: not "what is Q's position" but "redefine the chord so the coordinate Q actually has is the right one." It sidesteps the whole anchor-sampling apparatus. The cost is rebuilding the precompute over gaps instead of positions, which your pipeline can already do.
I'd actually rank it: try C first (it's the principled version of what you've already built and ships in a day), but hold E in reserve as the answer if C's signal is weak — because a weak C signal would most likely mean "position-mod-W overlap just isn't what attention wants here," and E directly tests the better hypothesis.

The real failure of dist-rope was that r scaled the only attention channel that existed, so high-mass substrings — the ones where AGPT has the most statistical evidence — got their signal driven to noise precisely where you most wanted it. H1: r lands on K only (scaled by r, not r²), content channel intact on Q. Survives.

Now the real question: is the depth shift load-bearing?
Your question 2 asks whether E3 alone captures enough that E4's shift is icing. I think the document actually contains the answer and then underweights it. Look at lines 183 and 213. You've already noticed that in AGPT, Q (depth k_q) and K (depth k_k, an ancestor) do not occur at the same corpus positions — they occur at positions separated by Δ = k_q − k_k. That's not a minor correction. It's structural and it's deterministic for the ancestor-descendant pairs that make up almost all of AGPT's attention.
Here's the consequence, made concrete. Suppose Q and K are a true ancestor-descendant pair on a path — say K = "th" and Q = "the", with Q one character deeper. Every single corpus occurrence of the path has Q's position exactly one greater than K's. So K's position distribution and Q's position distribution are the same distribution shifted by 1. Their phase chords are related by exactly e^{iω}.
Now compute E3's correlation, Re(conj(z_Q/C_Q)·z_K/C_K). Because the two distributions are offset by Δ=1, their chord directions at frequency ω differ by angle ω. For the low-frequency (long-period) dim-pairs, ω is tiny, the directions nearly align, correlation ≈ high. But for the high-frequency dim-pairs — the ones that actually localize position, the ones doing the real work — ω is order-1, the directions are substantially rotated apart, and the per-frequency correlation cos(Δω) swings toward zero or negative. So E3 systematically penalizes exactly the ancestor-descendant pairs it should be rewarding, and it does so worst at the frequencies that carry the most positional information.
That's not "E3 captures less signal." That's "E3 has the sign of the signal wrong at the informative frequencies." E4's e^{iΔω} is precisely the term that un-rotates that offset before correlating, so the high-frequency dim-pairs realign and the correlation peaks for genuine ancestor-descendant pairs. The shift isn't a refinement on top of a working E3; it's the thing that converts a near-zero-or-anticorrelated signal into a correctly-signed one.
So: load-bearing. E3 alone, I'd predict, gives you either no measurable improvement or a confusingly mixed one (helped by coincidental same-phase structure like indentation, hurt by the offset error on the dominant ancestor pairs). E3 is interesting only as a diagnostic baseline to confirm the shift matters — which actually answers your question 5 too, so I'll come back to it.
E4 vs my earlier "option E" (gap-distribution chord)
This is the comparison the document doesn't quite make, and it's worth making because they're the two principled ways to fix the coordinate mismatch and they have different cost/correctness profiles.
My earlier suggestion: build the chord over the gap distribution (distance to nearest prior occurrence) so that the chord lives natively in a relative coordinate, and Q's chunk-local depth — which is a relative offset — is already the right thing to probe with.
E4's approach: keep the absolute-position-mod-W chord, and apply the relative correction e^{iΔω} at correlation time.
They're not equivalent, and E4 is the better choice for you, for a reason that's easy to miss. The gap-distribution chord bakes in a single notion of "relative to what" (nearest prior occurrence) at precompute time, and it's lossy — once you've collapsed to gaps you can't recover finer structure. E4 keeps the full absolute-position chord and lets Δ be a free, per-pair quantity computed from trie metadata you already have. Different (Q,K) pairs in the same chunk get different, correct Δ's, all from one precompute. The gap chord would force you to recompute for each notion of offset. So E4 is strictly more flexible and reuses the dist-rope precompute you already have on disk (line 256). My option E would require a new precompute pass and throws away information. E4 wins; concede the point — it's the better version of the same fix.
The one thing the gap framing buys you that E4 doesn't get for free: numerical conditioning. More on that in the concerns.

Two more instruments worth wiring, both cheap:
The direct one is to log β's learned magnitude and its gradient over training. Concerns the document under-weights
A few things I'd flag before you commit the day:
The r_Q·r_K weighting may be too aggressive in the opposite direction now. You correctly moved r off the content channel. But E3/E4 still gate the bias by r_Q·r_K, which means the positional signal is only available for pairs where both substrings are sharp. The sharpest substrings are the rare/long ones — exactly the low-mass nodes where AGPT has the least statistical evidence and arguably needs the most help. Meanwhile "the"-class high-mass nodes, which appear in tons of contexts and where a good positional prior could be genuinely useful, get r ≈ 0 and no bias at all. This is the inverse of dist-rope's pathology but it's still a pathology: you've made the positional channel available precisely where it's least needed and absent where it might help most. Whether that's fine depends on whether you want positional structure for common substrings. I'd at least ablate a variant where the bias uses direction only (drop the r_Q·r_K gate, normalize each chord to unit magnitude before correlating) so the signal is available regardless of sharpness, and let β and the content channel sort out the weighting. Call it E4-norm. It's a one-line change and it directly tests whether the r gate is helping or just starving common substrings.
Numerical conditioning of low-mass chords. For singleton substrings (mass=1), z/C is a unit vector at one angle — fine. But the r you compute is |z|/C, and for very low counts the estimate of "coherence" is statistically meaningless (a substring seen twice is trivially "coherent" at every frequency). E4 will assign high r to noise. Given that ~most of your Gutenberg leaves past modest depth are near-singletons, a large fraction of your chords are high-r-by-undersampling. The r_Q·r_K gate will then preferentially fire the bias on the least statistically reliable pairs. This compounds the previous concern. A floor — ignore or down-weight chords below some count threshold, or shrink r toward zero with a count-dependent factor (a Bayesian/James-Stein style shrinkage on the coherence estimate) — would help. This is the one place the gap framing had an edge, because aggregating gaps pools evidence differently; but the cheaper fix is just count-shrinkage on r.
W's choice interacts with everything and isn't discussed. The chord is position-mod-W. W=64 is mentioned in the original doc, not justified here. If W is misaligned with the actual periodicities in the corpus (line length, etc.), the phase structure E3/E4 keys on is partly an artifact of the modulus. Worth a sensitivity check, or at least being aware that a null result might be a W problem, not a method problem.
