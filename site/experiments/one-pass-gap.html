The one-pass gap (AGPT pd=1–4 vs ordinary minibatch training)

AGPT's measuring stick is "one epoch and done". How close does AGPT get to a conventional training run in one pass over Tiny Shakespeare, and in a few more, against ordinary minibatch Adam training of the same d64/L2 model from the same weights? Is AGPT's deficit its update count: 65 per pass at pd=1, and more at pd=2–4 when the shallow targets are trained too? How does AGPT compare with conventional epochs, and how much of the trie's prefix sharing can a plan keep before the grouping it needs costs more than the sharing saves? Do AGPT's statistics help without its schedule, as trie targets in ordinary minibatch training, and at longer windows? And does a standard transformer trained on AGPT's exact steps reproduce AGPT?

THE ANSWER SO FAR

Far off, and the update count is only part of it. After one pass AGPT pd=1 scores rolling byte PPL 15.73; minibatch training scores 4.83 in 31 s, already better than AGPT's 100-pass run, and 4.66 after two passes, better than AGPT's best on record (4.764 after 625). More updates help AGPT a lot: one pass at pd=2 (1,463 updates) scores 9.65, pd=3 (12,675) 7.81 and pd=4 (58,721) 6.74. But random equal batches with the same update counts score 12.56, 5.18 and 4.83, and pd=4 takes 185 s a pass. At pd=3 the steps also match in average size; half the gap (0.27 of 0.48 nats) is the units' unequal sizes (random windows in batches of pd=3's sizes score 6.33), and half is the trie's grouping of each step by a shared prefix. Weighting units by mass (fire_norm sum) is worse (8.53). A conventional epoch, each character a target once (518 updates, 2 s), scores 6.94, better than a pd=3 pass, which computes 8.4 times as many positions; 8 conventional epochs, about a pd=3 pass's compute, score 5.12. In equal steps, the more of the trie's sharing a plan keeps, the worse it scores: 4.83 with 12% (random batches), 5.39 with 65%, 5.86 with 88%, 6.34 with 97%. Keeping 65% costs 0.11 nats, more than all of the sharing could save at this context length (about 0.05 nats at equal compute). AGPT's statistics do help without its schedule. With the trie's next-character distribution as each position's target, in the same batches, conventional training gains about 0.025 nats at 1–8 epochs (6.94 → 6.77 after one, 5.12 → 5.00 after eight), worth 1.13–1.37× the training, and 0.014–0.017 nats at 16 epochs. The gain halves at 32–64-character windows (about 0.013 nats) and is gone at 128: contexts stop repeating at about 12 characters. AGPT's equivalence holds: with an fp32 cache and TF32 off, v2's per-unit gradients match a float64 PyTorch transformer as closely as float32 PyTorch does, and replays with v2's units, order and events reproduce its runs within 0.4% (pd=1 15.72 against 15.73, pd=3 7.80 against 7.81). The normal v2 build's bf16 cache and TF32 put 1–4% errors into its unit gradients, up to 67% in some small units. At equal loss AGPT's plans lag random batches only at the shortest contexts (a unit's step gives a context the same weight however common it is), about 0.03 of pd=3's 0.48 nats. One seed; 188 runs.

one-pass-gap

Status: concluded (2026-11-14). The user asked for the experiment after we found the work had drifted off AGPT's main path. It is item 04 of the site's next tests ("Measure the one-pass gap"), never done before. The first grid was 48 runs in slot 30 (08:32–09:38 UTC). The follow-up, AGPT at minibatch cadence, was 47 runs in slot 33 (17:50–18:54 UTC). Follow-up 2, conventional epochs and AGPT's plans in the minibatch trainer, was 29 runs in slot 39 (2026-11-15, 01:29–02:07 UTC). Follow-up 3, trie targets in ordinary minibatch training, was 21 runs in slot 41 (02:56–03:11 UTC). Follow-ups 4 (trie targets at longer windows, 36 runs) and 5 (AGPT emulated exactly by a standard transformer: frozen-weight gradient comparisons and 7 replays) ran in slot 45 (07:50–08:35 UTC).

Question

AGPT's goal is a model that learns enough from one pass over a corpus to match a conventional training run, in less time. The record has AGPT's best d64/L2 result after 625 passes (rolling byte PPL 4.764, gradient-population adam-pd1-exact-625ep), and an ordinary minibatch run of the same model at 4.45 after 10 passes (June, vanilla-attn-torch, no result.json). Nobody has measured either method at one pass, at matched cost, under the canonical evaluation.

Three questions, in order:

  1. The gap at one pass: AGPT pd=1 after one trie traversal against minibatch training after one epoch, and against the conventional run.
  2. How many passes AGPT needs to reach what minibatch training reaches in one, and how many seconds that costs.
  3. Is it the update count? AGPT pd=1 takes 65 Adam steps per pass; minibatch training at 128 windows takes 8,279. The third arm is minibatch training with 16,302 windows per step, 65 steps per pass. If it tracks AGPT, AGPT's deficit is its cadence, as the context-tree orientation's no-trie control suggested (a tie with shuffled minibatches at equal steps). If AGPT beats it, the trie's grouping is worth something on its own.

Design

  • Model and data: d64 L2 h4 ff256, seq_len 16, Tiny Shakespeare canonical split 4fa9aec1db6b3aea; every run starts from the seed weights the AGPT record uses (rnd/cudax-d16-linear-mass-rerun/seeds/shake-d64L2-h4-dff256-d16-seed42.model, near-random: PPL 66). Seed 42.
  • One pass covers the same events in each arm. A depth-16 trie holds every prefix of length 1–16 with its next character; minibatch training visits every window start once per epoch with 16 targets per window.
  • AGPT arm (agpt-*): bin/agpt_train_v2, depth-16 trie, pd=1, exact ancestor gradient: the 625-pass record config with only the budget and learning rate changed.
  • Minibatch arms (mb-*, mbL-*): src/tools/agpt_minibatch_train.py (new, a port of the June trainer to an orchestrator custom trainer). mb: 128 windows per step. mbL: 16,302 windows per step, accumulated over micro-batches of 2,048 (same mean gradient).
  • Same optimizer and schedule everywhere: Adam (0.9, 0.999, eps 1e-8, no weight decay, the same as v2's kernel), warmup-cosine per optimizer step with no warmup, down to zero. The minibatch trainer reimplements v2's scheduled_lr step for step.
  • Each budget is its own run (1, 2, 4, 8 passes), so every run finishes its own schedule; intermediate checkpoints of a longer cosine run would not be the same thing.
  • Learning rates: AGPT and mbL at 0.003, 0.006, 0.012, 0.024; mb at 0.0015, 0.003, 0.006, and 0.012 added after 0.006 came out best at every budget. Every best value below is inside its grid.
  • Evaluation: the orchestrator's canonical lm-eval rolling byte PPL on the multi-chunk held-out split, plus its fixed-window PPL. Training seconds come from each result.json.
  • Cost: 44 runs, about an hour of GPU in one slot, most of it the 45 epochs of the mb arm. make_configs.py writes configs/; run_grid.sh rebuilds the binaries and runs them, one-pass runs first.

Checks done before the run (2026-11-14, CPU)

  • The trainer's model writer reproduces the seed file byte for byte after a load/save round trip.
  • A 2-epoch smoke run through bin/agpt_experiment produced a result.json with a scored epoch checkpoint and its training seconds; the learning rate at the end of epoch 1 of 2 was half the peak, as the cosine requires.
  • Micro-batch accumulation: 512 windows in one pass vs four of 128 gave the same training loss to 1e-5 and weights within 7e-4 (Adam amplifies rounding in near-zero gradients; the total change was 0.064).

Reading

  • AGPT is nowhere near one epoch and done at pd=1. One pass gives 15.7, against 4.83 for one pass of minibatch training. Minibatch training beats AGPT's 100-pass result in one pass and its best result ever (625 passes, about 70 minutes) in two passes (60 s).
  • Equal wall time doesn't rescue it. An AGPT pass is about 4× cheaper here (7 s against 31 s), but AGPT after 54 s (8 passes) is at 8.71, where minibatch training was at 4.83 after 31 s.
  • The update count explains most of the gap. With AGPT's 65 steps per pass, random equal batches score 12.6 after one pass, much nearer to AGPT's 15.7 than to minibatch training's 4.8. Each step there averages 16,302 windows, so it is close to the exact full-batch gradient, and 65 such steps can't train the model in one pass however exact they are.
  • But AGPT's 65 steps are worth less than 65 random batches, and the shortfall grows with training: 15.7 against 12.6 after one pass, 8.71 against 5.67 after eight. AGPT after 8 passes (520 steps) sits between the control's 2 and 4 passes (130 and 260 steps), so its steps are worth about a third of the control's. Three things differ and are not yet separated:
    1. Unit sizes: AGPT's 65 units are the root characters' subtrees, from the space-rooted one down to a handful of events, and Adam takes a full-size step on each. The control's batches are equal.
    2. Order: v2 trains the units in the same fixed order every pass (experimental.unit_order_seed defaults to −1), so each pass ends on the same rare letters at the bottom of the cosine. The control shuffles.
    3. Grouping: each unit holds the contexts that start with one character. The context-tree orientation control found that grouping costs nothing for a GRU against random groups of the same unequal sizes in shuffled order, so it is the least likely of the three, but it is untested with attention.
  • Learning rates: AGPT's best rate falls from 0.012 to 0.006 as the budget grows, and 0.006 is best for minibatch training at every budget (the 625-pass record used 0.0015). The control's best is 0.006–0.012.

Follow-up: AGPT at minibatch cadence (2026-11-14)

The user chose next test 2 ("Sure do pd=3. The main issue is the number of updates."). One pass of pd=1 is 65 updates. pd=3 cuts the trie into one unit per depth-3 node, which gives more updates per pass than minibatch training takes. 47 runs in slot 33 (17:50–18:54 UTC).

The fix it needs. A pd≥2 plan trains each anchor's subtree, with the anchor's ancestors as context only, so the targets of every node whose edge ends above depth pd were never trained. v2 now prints a plan-coverage line. On the canonical trie, without the fix:

  • pd=2 leaves 62 nodes untrained, 6.25% of the events;
  • pd=3 leaves 1,194 nodes, 12.43%;
  • pd=4 leaves 8,738 nodes, 18.41%.

The new option experimental.train_crown (b1019b3) makes each such crown node a unit of its own, with its ancestors as context. With it, every one of the 1,528,495 radix nodes is trained exactly once per pass at pd=2, 3 and 4, as at pd=1 (checked on the CPU with --mode plan, and in each run's train.log).

Arms. Same model, weights, schedule and evaluation as above; configs from make_configs.py:

  • pd2c, pd3c, pd4c: AGPT at pd=2, 3 and 4 with crown units, in radix-id order (v2's default; crown units fall among the anchored ones).
  • pd3cs: pd=3, shuffled each pass (experimental.unit_order_seed: 1), at 1, 2 and 4 passes.
  • mb724, mb84: minibatch training with as many updates per pass as pd=2 (724 windows, 1,464 updates) and pd=3 (84 windows, 12,615 updates). mb84's batches hold 1,344 events, and pd=3's units 1,338 on average.
  • mbu3: random windows in batches with pd=3's 12,675 unit sizes, in a new order each pass (experimental.step_events_file, 21ec506). The sizes are each unit's trained events from the pd3cs lr 0.0015 log (pd3_unit_events.txt); a step takes max(1, round(events / 16)) windows.
  • pd1s, pd3css: pd=1, and shuffled pd=3, with train.fire_norm: sum (e59b10c). v2's default divides each unit's gradient by its own event count, so the updates of a pass add up to Σ_u mean_u(g) and weight every unit equally, whatever its size. sum divides every unit by one constant, so a pass weights every event equally, as the corpus loss does.

pd=3's best one-pass rate came out at 0.0015, the bottom of its first grid (0.0015–0.012), so the grid was extended down to 0.000375 before the rest ran. All one-pass bests below are inside their grids except pd1s.

Reading

  • More updates help AGPT a lot, but they don't close the gap. One pass falls from 15.7 at 65 updates to 9.65 at 1,463, 7.81 at 12,675 and 6.74 at 58,721. Random equal batches reach 5.18 with 1,464 updates and 4.83 from 8,279 on. AGPT is far behind at every matched count. pd=4, with 40 times mb724's updates, still trails it (6.74 against 5.18).
  • At pd=3 the gap is in what each step holds. The step count is matched, and so is the average step size, so the gap of 0.48 nats (ln 7.81 − ln 4.83) comes from the steps' contents. It splits about in half:
    • Unequal sizes cost 0.27 nats (4.83 → 6.33, random windows in batches of pd=3's sizes). pd=3's median unit holds 168 events, 2,005 units hold fewer than 24, and the largest holds 212,674, 1.25% of a pass. Under the event-mean rule, Adam gives every one of them a step of about the same size.
    • The trie's grouping costs another 0.21 nats (6.33 → 7.81), against random windows in batches of the same sizes. This is the cost of giving each step only contexts that share their first three characters. The context-tree orientation control found no such cost at pd=1 with a GRU (first-character groups against random groups of the same sizes); at pd=3 with attention the cost is there.
  • Weighting the units by their mass makes it worse. fire_norm: sum scores 8.53 against 7.81 at pd=3. Under Adam, summed gradients give the few big units most of the movement and the thousands of small ones almost none, which cuts the number of updates that count. The size problem calls for units of equal size, not reweighted gradients.
  • Order matters little: shuffled 7.81, radix-id order 8.00.
  • Deeper partitions also cost time. With the exact ancestor gradient, a pd=3 pass takes 53 s and a pd=4 pass 185 s, against 31 s (128 windows) and 45 s (84 windows) for a minibatch pass. So at these depths AGPT is slower per pass as well as worse.
  • Answer to the user's point: the number of updates is necessary. AGPT improves steadily with it, and at pd=1 it is most of the gap. But beyond about 1,500 updates per pass, the composition of each update matters more than how many there are.

Follow-up 2: conventional epochs, and AGPT's plans in the minibatch trainer (2026-11-15)

Two questions the record had never asked. Every "epoch" above, in v2 and in the minibatch trainer, is every window start with 16 targets each, so each character is a target 16 times per pass. A conventional epoch makes each character a target once. How does AGPT compare with that? And the trie saves compute only where a step's contexts share prefixes, which means grouping them. How much of the sharing can a plan keep before the grouping costs more than the sharing saves? 29 runs in slot 39 (2026-11-15, 01:29–02:07 UTC), code bf32854.

Arms. Same model, seed weights, Adam, schedule and evaluation as above, all in src/tools/agpt_minibatch_train.py.

  • cv: conventional epochs (experimental.window_stride: 16): 66,226 non-overlapping windows from a random offset each epoch, 1,059,616 targets, 128 windows a step, 518 steps per epoch. Run for 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 epochs. Sixteen epochs compute as many positions as one pass of mb.
  • tp3: AGPT's pd=3 + crown plan, replayed event by event (experimental.trie_plan: pd, new module src/tools/trie_plan.py). Each unit is one Adam step on its own events, picked out of ordinary windows by a loss mask, and the units are shuffled each pass. A unit's gradient is the sum of its events' gradients, so this gives the updates v2 gives, computed by a different program. It is the control for everything between v2 and the PyTorch trainer.
  • tq1338, tq335, tq84: packed plans (trie_plan: packed), each with 12,675 steps of equal size and 1,338 events per step on average, as at pd=3. A window's deep events go to a piece: the subtree of the shallowest prefix that holds at most P events (P = 1,338, 335 or 84). The heavier nodes' own events are split by occurrence into chunks of at most P. Pieces and chunks are packed at random into the steps, repacked each pass. The smaller P is, the shallower the shared prefixes and the less sharing is left.

Trie positions per pass count what a trie trainer would compute for a plan: in each step, every distinct context its events read. The replay trainer itself computes whole windows, so its times don't show the sharing. Every pass has 16,953,872 events.

Checks done before the run (CPU):

  • With no trie plan the trainer writes a byte-identical model to before.
  • The pd=3 replay has v2's 12,675 units, and 99.5% of the sorted sizes are identical to v2's. The total differs by 272 events, from the trie's wrap at the corpus end.
  • Every event is trained exactly once per pass in every plan.
  • Windows cut at their deepest event give the full-window gradient to within 8e-16 (float64).

Reading

  • The control holds. The pd=3 replay scores 7.76 against v2's 7.81, at the same best rate (0.0015; the neighbours are 7.94 and 8.32, against v2's 8.13 and 8.01). So the pd=3 gap is in the plan, not in v2.
  • One conventional epoch beats a pass of AGPT pd=3. 518 steps and 1.06M positions (2 s) give 6.94. AGPT's pd=3 pass takes 24 times the steps and 8.4 times the positions and gives 7.76–7.81. Only pd=4 (6.74, 185 s) is better than one conventional epoch.
  • At equal compute the gap is wide. Eight conventional epochs compute about as many positions as a pd=3 pass (8.48M against 8.93M) and score 5.12 against 7.81.
  • The every-start objective is worth what it costs, no more. Sixteen conventional epochs score 4.85 and one pass over every window start 4.83, with the same positions and nearly the same steps (8,288 against 8,279). Training each target at all 16 context lengths is as good as seeing the corpus 16 times.
  • The more sharing a plan keeps, the worse it scores. With equal steps and equal mean size, keeping 12% of the sharing gives 4.83, 65% 5.39, 88% 5.86 and 97% 6.34.
  • Equal sizes fix the size half and leave the grouping half. At equal sizes, grouping costs 0.27 nats (4.83 → 6.34). pd=3's unequal sizes on top of that grouping cost 0.20 more (6.34 → 7.76). In follow-up 1, in the other order, the two costs were 0.27 and 0.21 nats, so they roughly add. Unequal sizes without grouping (mbu3, 6.33) cost the same as grouping at equal sizes (tq1338, 6.34).
  • No point on the curve pays. tq84 computes 11.71M positions a pass in a trie trainer and scores 5.39. Conventional epochs with that many positions score about 5.0, interpolated between 8 and 16 epochs, and that is before the trie trainer's per-position cost (v2 is about 2.2× slower per position than the PyTorch trainer). At the other end, if the full sharing cost nothing in step quality, pd=3's 8.93M positions would buy random-batch quality (4.83) where conventional training at that compute scores about 5.10. That is a gain of about 0.05 nats. Keeping even 65% of the sharing costs 0.11 nats (5.39 against 4.83).
  • Why the gain is that small. The trie's saving is a fixed ~8.2M positions, about 7.7 per character, all from contexts shorter than about 12 characters: past that depth almost every context in the corpus is distinct. So a step can share only where its contexts have a short prefix in common. At longer context lengths the saving is a smaller share of the cost (1.32× at T=32, 1.06× at T=128), so the 0.05-nat ceiling shrinks.

Follow-up 3: trie targets at minibatch cadence (2026-11-15)

Follow-up 2 found that the trie loses as a training schedule. AGPT has a second part, its statistics. Each node's loss is n(c) · H(n(c, ·)/n(c), q_θ(· | c)), the cross-entropy against the full distribution of characters that follow context c in the corpus. This arm keeps ordinary minibatch training and replaces each position's one-hot target with that distribution (experimental.trie_targets, 2ffa5d9). Summed over a context's occurrences, the one-hot targets equal n(c) times the distribution. So the expected gradient is unchanged, the variance is lower, and no steps are lost: AGPT's node loss without AGPT's schedule. The user chose it on 2026-11-15. 21 runs in slot 41 (02:56–03:11 UTC).

Arms. cvt: the conventional epochs of follow-up 2 with trie targets, at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 epochs. mbt: one pass over every window start (mb) with trie targets. cv at 16 epochs gained two rates (0.003, 0.012) to match. The trie targets don't touch the random generator, so each cvt or mbt run sees the same batches in the same order as the cv or mb run at its rate. The pairs differ only in their targets.

Checks done before the run (CPU):

  • The target table (trie_plan.context_targets: 8,902,737 contexts, 9,937,989 context–next pairs) matches a brute-force count for 300 random events. 8,396,412 of the contexts are always followed by the same character and so keep a one-hot target; 7,919,695 of those are seen only once. (The train logs call the 8,396,412 "seen once"; that label was wrong.)
  • Over every event the soft and one-hot full-batch gradients agree to 1.8e-15 (float64, a 6,000-character slice). The objective is unchanged.
  • With the option off, all three default modes write byte-identical models.
  • Gradient noise of a 128-window batch, measured from 64 random batches: trie targets cut it by 19% at the seed weights, 31% after one conventional epoch and 38% after sixteen. A rough critical-batch estimate (noise over squared mean gradient) is about 13 windows at the seed weights and thousands after the first epoch, so batch 128 is noise-limited for most of training. From that I predicted the targets would be worth about 1.5× the training (one epoch near 6.4).

Reading

  • AGPT's statistics help even though its schedule doesn't. With the same steps and the same batches, the trie's targets give about 0.025 nats from 1 to 8 epochs. That is worth 1.13× the training at one epoch, rising to 1.37× at eight.
  • The gain is smaller than predicted, and smallest early. The prediction was about 1.5× from the start; one epoch gives 1.13×. Early steps are not noise-limited: at the seed weights the mean gradient dominates, and the targets cut the noise there by only 19%. The relative worth grows as training becomes noise-limited (31–38% less noise).
  • The gain in nats shrinks with longer training (0.017 at 16 epochs, 0.014 for the every-start pass). The objective's optimum is the same, so with enough training the gain should go away. It is a speed-up, not a better model.
  • Cost: in this implementation, building each step's targets adds about 10% to an epoch (1.97 s against 1.80 s), and the table takes 2–3 s per run. At equal training seconds, building excluded, one-hot targets get 1.1× the epochs and score about 6.82, 5.96, 5.43 and 5.09 at 1, 2, 4 and 8 epochs. So the trie targets keep about a quarter of their gain at one epoch and 70% at eight.
  • It reaches only contexts that repeat. 8.40M of the 8.90M contexts are always followed by the same character and keep one-hot targets. The events whose targets change are those with contexts shorter than about 12 characters, about half the events at T=16. At longer context lengths that share falls (the same bound as the trie's compute saving in follow-up 2), so the gain should shrink; follow-up 4 measures it.
  • For comparison: the 1 GB count prior in the 10.8M PG-19 model's input and logits gained 0.025 bits/char (0.017 nats) at 50k steps (prior-scaling-plain-1g). The count-backoff-gate sidecar, smoothed counts as targets, cost 0.23 nats (6.70 against 5.30). Raw counts keep the objective; smoothed ones change it.

Follow-up 4: trie targets at longer windows (2026-11-15)

Follow-up 3's gain comes from contexts that repeat, and almost none do past about 12 characters. This follow-up asks how much of the gain survives at longer windows. The user asked for it on 2026-11-15.

Arms. cv{T} and cvt{T}: follow-up 3's conventional epochs, one-hot and trie targets, at seq_len T = 32, 64 and 128. Every step takes 2,048 targets (64, 32 or 16 windows), so every T takes 518 steps an epoch, as T=16 does with 128 windows. 1 and 4 epochs at lr 0.003, 0.006 and 0.012, paired as before. Both eval metrics follow the model's window: rolling PPL in windows of T, fixed-window PPL with T characters of context. So numbers compare across arms at one T, not across T.

Code (a712cad). The target table now lists only contexts seen at two or more window starts; a context seen once takes its own next character. At T=16 its rows are bitwise identical to the old table's (50 batches of 128), at T=64 they match a brute-force count (300 random events), and at T=128 it builds in 10 s with 1.8 GB peak memory.

Checks done before the run (CPU). The share of events whose context repeats, and the gradient-noise cut per 2,048-target batch at the seed weights (64 random batches each):

T Events with a repeated context Noise cut at seed

By depth, 99% of contexts repeat at 4 characters, 58% at 8, 16% at 12, 5% at 16 and 1% at 24. Reading

  • The gain shrinks with the window, as expected, but more slowly than predicted. About 0.014 nats at T=32 and 0.013 at T=64, roughly half of T=16's, and every pair improves. At T=128 it is gone: the pairs split 3 to 3 and the mean is 0.001. The prediction (0.011, 0.005, 0.002) was right at the ends and low at T=64 by more than half, which in one seed may be noise.
  • So it is bounded the way the trie's compute saving is. Contexts stop repeating at about 12 characters whatever the window, so the targets that change are a fixed number per window (about 8.7 of them), and their share of the training falls as 1/T. At ordinary LM context lengths (hundreds or thousands of tokens) the gain from exact counts is nil. To matter there, statistics would have to cover far more context than an exact trie does.

  • Numbers compare across arms at one T, not across T: both eval metrics follow the window.

Follow-up 5: AGPT emulated exactly by a standard transformer (2026-11-15)

The user's point (2026-11-15, passed on by @air): AGPT's equivalence is exact, so a standard transformer emulating AGPT's run should give essentially the same results; if it doesn't, something is wrong. The check they suggested: freeze the weights, compute the same batch's gradient both ways, compare relative error and cosine.

The record had checked v2's gradient only against finite differences of v2's own loss (gradient-population, Experiment 8), which shows its backward matches its forward, and had tp3's outcome-level match to pd3cs at the best rate. Neither compared v2 with a standard transformer, and the replays were not v2's runs step for step:

  • Order. The replay shuffled units with its own generator, so tp3 and pd3cs take the same 12,675 units in different orders.
  • 272 events. The cached trie wraps the corpus tail to the front (boundary_mode: tail-wrap-front-v1): a window starts at every character, 1,059,634 × 16 = 16,954,144 events, which is v2's count. The replay took only windows that fit, 272 events fewer. With the wrap, the window counts reproduce all 65 of v2's pd=1 unit sizes; without it, 14 differ.

v2's Adam is the standard update (adam_bulk_kernel, src/cuda/kernels.cu), and the minibatch trainer already copied v2's learning-rate schedule.

Built (99b3387): src/tools/agpt_unit_grad_compare.py, which recomputes each unit of a frozen v2 gradient dump (AGPT_GRAD_DUMP_DIR) in the PyTorch model over the same events, in float64, and reports relative error and cosine per unit, per tensor and for the whole pass, with a float32 PyTorch gradient as the rounding floor of an ordinary implementation. Trainer options wrap_tail (v2's events), trie_plan_order_file (v2's units in v2's order, from a dump's units.tsv, checked unit by unit against the plan's event counts) and log_step_loss. Arms tp1x (pd=1) and tp3x (pd=3 + crown, shuffled), run with v2's orders in v2_units/. The dumps' unit order is the order the original runs trained in: both sequences match their train.logs unit for unit.

The work ran in slot 45 (run_emulation.sh, then extra_dumps.sh): twelve frozen dumps, 7 replay runs. Comparisons on CPU afterwards; grad_compare.json has every summary.

Where the grouped runs fall behind

The user read these results (the math holds, so the schedule is what costs) as learning by contrast. A step whose windows share a prefix has no contrast at the root, so the root region would change slowly while the leaves are updated often. If so, the grouped runs should be furthest behind at the shortest contexts.

At equal steps the gap grows with context length, but that says nothing about grouping. Held-out loss by context length (depth_loss.py, every window start in the held-out chunks, best-rate run of each arm), gap in nats to the random-batch run with the same steps:

The pd=1 runs are less trained than every random-batch run on record (2.75 at contexts 5–16 against at most 2.66), so they have no reference.

Why: a unit's step gives a context the same weight however common it is. Each step is the mean over its events, so over a pass a context gets the sum, over the steps that hold it, of its share of each step. In random batches that is its count over the batch size: weight in proportion to frequency, which is what the corpus loss asks for. In a pd≥2 plan each 1-character context is a crown unit of its own, so it gets exactly one step. The commonest, a space (161,324 occurrences), gets 1 step at pd=3 where random batches of the same size give it 121 steps' worth; 1 against 14 at pd=2 and 1 against 559 at pd=4, and the lag grows in that order. The packed plans split a heavy node's events into chunks of at most P and spread them over the pass, which gives the 1-character contexts about 790 steps' worth at P = 1,338, as random batches do (792), while keeping 97% of the sharing; their root lag is gone. Their chunks are still one context each, so what the root lacked was training weight, not variety within a step.

The mass does weight each node, but only inside its unit: a unit's gradient is the sum of its events' gradients, and v2's default (fire_norm: mass) then divides it by the unit's own mass, which makes every unit's step the same size. For a crown unit, a single node, that division cancels its count entirely. Carrying the mass across units (fire_norm: sum) takes most of the root lag away (+0.08 at one character), but the run is worse overall (8.53 against 7.81, and 2.10 against 1.97 at contexts 5–16): Adam scales each step by the recent size of the gradients, not by this step's, so the big units fill that scale and the thousands of small ones barely move. Mass can weight events within an Adam step but not one step against another. Steps of equal mass, as in the packed plans, avoid the choice.

At pd=1 there is no crown: a 1-character context is 1/16 of its unit, and its whole unit carries the same reweighting (windows that start with a space are 15% of the events and get 1/65 of the steps), so it falls on every depth of those windows, not on the root. At pd=0 the one step's weights are the frequencies exactly: full-batch training.

The root lag is a small part of the gap: about 0.03 of the 0.48 nats between pd=3 and random batches. Outside the shortest contexts the profiles match, so the rest is slower learning at every context length, from the unequal unit sizes (mbu3, first follow-up) and the grouping by prefix (the packed plans, follow-up 2).

Reading

  • The equivalence holds. In full precision v2's gradients match a standard transformer's to rounding, as closely as PyTorch's own float32 does, at both weight points and both plans. Replays with v2's units, order and events reproduce v2's runs to within 0.4% at every rate. AGPT's math is not what costs it the one-pass gap: the schedule is, which events share a step.
  • The normal v2 build is not exact. bf16 keys and values and TF32 matmuls each put errors of 1–4% into its per-unit gradients (0.2% on the whole pass at the seed, 8% trained), and up to 67% in some small shallow units. In one pass that does not change the outcome (the true-float32 replays match), but every AGPT run on record used this build. It may matter for long runs and for curvature methods, which need accurate gradients.
  • The old replay's gaps at neighbouring rates were the order. In v2's order the replay is within 0.4%; in its own order it was 2–4% off (7.94 against 8.13, 8.32 against 8.01). Runs that differ only in unit order differ by a few percent, so one seed resolves differences between plans only above that.

Caveats

  • One seed. The seed weights are the AGPT record's; in June they slowed minibatch training compared with random init.
  • AGPT's 65 pd=1 units differ in size (by root character); mbL's 65 steps are equal random batches.
  • Equal passes is not equal compute: training seconds are reported for each run so the comparison can be read either way. They time the trainer process, start-up included: about 1 s for AGPT (loading the trie; a pass is about 6.6 s) and about 2 s for the minibatch trainer (starting PyTorch, reading the corpus; a pass is 28.6 s).
  • Each run's meta.json says git_dirty: true: the trainer and the configs were committed (84054bc) before the runs, but untracked files (this README's summary script, the grid's output) and, for the four lr 0.012 runs, the edited make_configs.py were in the tree.
  • Follow-up runs are dirty for the same reasons: untracked weight files elsewhere in rnd/, the grid's logs, and configs written between commits. The trainer code is the commit each meta.json names: each restart of the grid rebuilt both binaries after a commit, and the one build that came before later edits (18:02:32 UTC, for the runs naming b1019b3) finished a minute before the first of them.
  • Follow-up: one seed. The 2- and 4-pass pd=3 runs tried two rates each, and 0.0015, the higher, won both, so more passes may do somewhat better at a higher rate. pd1s's best one-pass rate is the bottom of its grid.
  • mbu3 matches pd=3's step sizes, not its mix of depths: a window gives one target at each depth 1–16, while a pd=3 unit's targets sit at the depths of its subtree. Rounding to whole windows gives 1,060,418 windows per pass, 801 more than one epoch; the extra ones come from a second random permutation.
  • Follow-up 2: one seed. tq1338's best rate is the bottom of its grid. The 16-epoch cv run tried only 0.006, the best rate at every other budget.
  • The plan replays' training seconds in result.json include building the plan (6–17 s). The replay trainer computes whole windows for every step, so its times say nothing about what a trie trainer would take. Positions per pass are the cost measure for the plans.
  • Follow-up 3: one seed, but paired: each trie-target run has the same initial weights and the same batches as its one-hot partner.
  • Follow-up 2 and 3 runs say git_dirty: true because of untracked files only (weight files elsewhere in rnd/, the grid logs). The tracked tree was the commit each meta.json names (bf32854, 2ffa5d9).
  • Follow-up 4: one seed, paired like follow-up 3, three rates and two budgets per window length. The T=64 gain is above the prediction by more than half; whether T=32 and T=64 really differ is within one seed's noise.
  • Follow-up 5: the pd=3 comparisons cover 300 of 12,675 units, at two weight points. The dumps and comparisons are diagnostics outside the orchestrator (grad_compare.json, built from the tool's output); the replays are orchestrator runs. The replays' training seconds include logging every step's loss and building the plan, so they say nothing about speed. The v2_units/ orders were written in the slot before the replays ran and committed after, so those runs' meta.json (99b3387, git_dirty: true) predates them; each replay's train.log records its order file's sha256, which matches the committed file.
  • Loss by context length (depth_loss.py, depth_matched.py) is a CPU diagnostic on the runs' checkpoints, one seed. The matched-loss reference is linear interpolation between random-batch runs, with no run between 1.69 and 1.87 at contexts 5–16 (where the pd=4 runs sit) or between 2.07 and 2.52 (the pd=2 runs). The 1-character reference rises only 0.05 and 0.11 across those gaps, so if it rises steadily the root excess is at least 0.60 at pd=4 and 0.18 at pd=2 whatever its shape there. The pd=3 runs sit between reference runs 0.16 apart.
