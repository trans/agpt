The one-pass gap (AGPT pd=1–4 vs ordinary minibatch training)

AGPT's measuring stick is "one epoch and done". How close does AGPT get to a conventional training run in one pass over Tiny Shakespeare, and in a few more, against ordinary minibatch Adam training of the same d64/L2 model from the same weights? Is AGPT's deficit its update count: 65 per pass at pd=1, and more at pd=2–4 when the shallow targets are trained too?

experimentconcludednegativeattentioneval: canonical
Opened 2026-11-14Updated 2026-11-14
THE ANSWER SO FAR

Far off, and the update count is only part of it. After one pass AGPT pd=1 scores rolling byte PPL 15.73; minibatch training scores 4.83 in 31 s, already better than AGPT's 100-pass run, and 4.66 after two passes, better than AGPT's best on record (4.764 after 625). More updates help AGPT a lot: one pass at pd=2 (1,463 updates) scores 9.65, pd=3 (12,675) 7.81 and pd=4 (58,721) 6.74. But random equal batches with the same update counts score 12.56, 5.18 and 4.83, and pd=4 takes 185 s a pass. At pd=3 the steps also match in average size; half the gap (0.27 of 0.48 nats) is the units' unequal sizes (random windows in batches of pd=3's sizes score 6.33), and half is the trie's grouping of each step by a shared prefix. Weighting units by mass (fire_norm sum) is worse (8.53). One seed; 95 runs.

one-pass-gap

Status: concluded (2026-11-14). The user asked for the experiment after we found the work had drifted off AGPT's main path. It is item 04 of the site's next tests ("Measure the one-pass gap"), never done before. The first grid was 48 runs in slot 30 (08:32–09:38 UTC). The follow-up, AGPT at minibatch cadence, was 47 runs in slot 33 (17:50–18:54 UTC).

Question

AGPT's goal is a model that learns enough from one pass over a corpus to match a conventional training run, in less time. The record has AGPT's best d64/L2 result after 625 passes (rolling byte PPL 4.764, gradient-population adam-pd1-exact-625ep), and an ordinary minibatch run of the same model at 4.45 after 10 passes (June, vanilla-attn-torch, no result.json). Nobody has measured either method at one pass, at matched cost, under the canonical evaluation.

Three questions, in order:

  1. The gap at one pass: AGPT pd=1 after one trie traversal against minibatch training after one epoch, and against the conventional run.
  2. How many passes AGPT needs to reach what minibatch training reaches in one, and how many seconds that costs.
  3. Is it the update count? AGPT pd=1 takes 65 Adam steps per pass; minibatch training at 128 windows takes 8,279. The third arm is minibatch training with 16,302 windows per step, 65 steps per pass. If it tracks AGPT, AGPT's deficit is its cadence, as the context-tree orientation's no-trie control suggested (a tie with shuffled minibatches at equal steps). If AGPT beats it, the trie's grouping is worth something on its own.

Design

  • Model and data: d64 L2 h4 ff256, seq_len 16, Tiny Shakespeare canonical split 4fa9aec1db6b3aea; every run starts from the seed weights the AGPT record uses (rnd/cudax-d16-linear-mass-rerun/seeds/shake-d64L2-h4-dff256-d16-seed42.model, near-random: PPL 66). Seed 42.
  • One pass covers the same events in each arm. A depth-16 trie holds every prefix of length 1–16 with its next character; minibatch training visits every window start once per epoch with 16 targets per window.
  • AGPT arm (agpt-*): bin/agpt_train_v2, depth-16 trie, pd=1, exact ancestor gradient: the 625-pass record config with only the budget and learning rate changed.
  • Minibatch arms (mb-*, mbL-*): src/tools/agpt_minibatch_train.py (new, a port of the June trainer to an orchestrator custom trainer). mb: 128 windows per step. mbL: 16,302 windows per step, accumulated over micro-batches of 2,048 (same mean gradient).
  • Same optimizer and schedule everywhere: Adam (0.9, 0.999, eps 1e-8, no weight decay, the same as v2's kernel), warmup-cosine per optimizer step with no warmup, down to zero. The minibatch trainer reimplements v2's scheduled_lr step for step.
  • Each budget is its own run (1, 2, 4, 8 passes), so every run finishes its own schedule; intermediate checkpoints of a longer cosine run would not be the same thing.
  • Learning rates: AGPT and mbL at 0.003, 0.006, 0.012, 0.024; mb at 0.0015, 0.003, 0.006, and 0.012 added after 0.006 came out best at every budget. Every best value below is inside its grid.
  • Evaluation: the orchestrator's canonical lm-eval rolling byte PPL on the multi-chunk held-out split, plus its fixed-window PPL. Training seconds come from each result.json.
  • Cost: 44 runs, about an hour of GPU in one slot, most of it the 45 epochs of the mb arm. make_configs.py writes configs/; run_grid.sh rebuilds the binaries and runs them, one-pass runs first.

Checks done before the run (2026-11-14, CPU)

  • The trainer's model writer reproduces the seed file byte for byte after a load/save round trip.
  • A 2-epoch smoke run through bin/agpt_experiment produced a result.json with a scored epoch checkpoint and its training seconds; the learning rate at the end of epoch 1 of 2 was half the peak, as the cosine requires.
  • Micro-batch accumulation: 512 windows in one pass vs four of 128 gave the same training loss to 1e-5 and weights within 7e-4 (Adam amplifies rounding in near-zero gradients; the total change was 0.064).

Reading

  • AGPT is nowhere near one epoch and done at pd=1. One pass gives 15.7, against 4.83 for one pass of minibatch training. Minibatch training beats AGPT's 100-pass result in one pass and its best result ever (625 passes, about 70 minutes) in two passes (60 s).
  • Equal wall time doesn't rescue it. An AGPT pass is about 4× cheaper here (7 s against 31 s), but AGPT after 54 s (8 passes) is at 8.71, where minibatch training was at 4.83 after 31 s.
  • The update count explains most of the gap. With AGPT's 65 steps per pass, random equal batches score 12.6 after one pass, much nearer to AGPT's 15.7 than to minibatch training's 4.8. Each step there averages 16,302 windows, so it is close to the exact full-batch gradient, and 65 such steps can't train the model in one pass however exact they are.
  • But AGPT's 65 steps are worth less than 65 random batches, and the shortfall grows with training: 15.7 against 12.6 after one pass, 8.71 against 5.67 after eight. AGPT after 8 passes (520 steps) sits between the control's 2 and 4 passes (130 and 260 steps), so its steps are worth about a third of the control's. Three things differ and are not yet separated:
    1. Unit sizes: AGPT's 65 units are the root characters' subtrees, from the space-rooted one down to a handful of events, and Adam takes a full-size step on each. The control's batches are equal.
    2. Order: v2 trains the units in the same fixed order every pass (experimental.unit_order_seed defaults to −1), so each pass ends on the same rare letters at the bottom of the cosine. The control shuffles.
    3. Grouping: each unit holds the contexts that start with one character. The context-tree orientation control found that grouping costs nothing for a GRU against random groups of the same unequal sizes in shuffled order, so it is the least likely of the three, but it is untested with attention.
  • Learning rates: AGPT's best rate falls from 0.012 to 0.006 as the budget grows, and 0.006 is best for minibatch training at every budget (the 625-pass record used 0.0015). The control's best is 0.006–0.012.

Next tests (not run)

  1. What makes AGPT's steps weaker (about 15 minutes of GPU): the minibatch trainer grouping windows by their first character, which is AGPT pd=1's units in minibatch form, in v2's fixed order and shuffled; random groups with the units' sizes; and v2 with experimental.unit_order_seed set. The fixed-order first-character arm should reproduce AGPT's numbers, which would also check that AGPT pd=1 is grouped SGD in practice.
  2. AGPT at minibatch cadence: done, in the follow-up below.

Follow-up: AGPT at minibatch cadence (2026-11-14)

The user chose next test 2 ("Sure do pd=3. The main issue is the number of updates."). One pass of pd=1 is 65 updates. pd=3 cuts the trie into one unit per depth-3 node, which gives more updates per pass than minibatch training takes. 47 runs in slot 33 (17:50–18:54 UTC).

The fix it needs. A pd≥2 plan trains each anchor's subtree, with the anchor's ancestors as context only, so the targets of every node whose edge ends above depth pd were never trained. v2 now prints a plan-coverage line. On the canonical trie, without the fix:

  • pd=2 leaves 62 nodes untrained, 6.25% of the events;
  • pd=3 leaves 1,194 nodes, 12.43%;
  • pd=4 leaves 8,738 nodes, 18.41%.

The new option experimental.train_crown (b1019b3) makes each such crown node a unit of its own, with its ancestors as context. With it, every one of the 1,528,495 radix nodes is trained exactly once per pass at pd=2, 3 and 4, as at pd=1 (checked on the CPU with --mode plan, and in each run's train.log).

Arms. Same model, weights, schedule and evaluation as above; configs from make_configs.py:

  • pd2c, pd3c, pd4c: AGPT at pd=2, 3 and 4 with crown units, in radix-id order (v2's default; crown units fall among the anchored ones).
  • pd3cs: pd=3, shuffled each pass (experimental.unit_order_seed: 1), at 1, 2 and 4 passes.
  • mb724, mb84: minibatch training with as many updates per pass as pd=2 (724 windows, 1,464 updates) and pd=3 (84 windows, 12,615 updates). mb84's batches hold 1,344 events, and pd=3's units 1,338 on average.
  • mbu3: random windows in batches with pd=3's 12,675 unit sizes, in a new order each pass (experimental.step_events_file, 21ec506). The sizes are each unit's trained events from the pd3cs lr 0.0015 log (pd3_unit_events.txt); a step takes max(1, round(events / 16)) windows.
  • pd1s, pd3css: pd=1, and shuffled pd=3, with train.fire_norm: sum (e59b10c). v2's default divides each unit's gradient by its own event count, so the updates of a pass add up to Σ_u mean_u(g) and weight every unit equally, whatever its size. sum divides every unit by one constant, so a pass weights every event equally, as the corpus loss does.

pd=3's best one-pass rate came out at 0.0015, the bottom of its first grid (0.0015–0.012), so the grid was extended down to 0.000375 before the rest ran. All one-pass bests below are inside their grids except pd1s.

Reading

  • More updates help AGPT a lot, but they don't close the gap. One pass falls from 15.7 at 65 updates to 9.65 at 1,463, 7.81 at 12,675 and 6.74 at 58,721. Random equal batches reach 5.18 with 1,464 updates and 4.83 from 8,279 on. AGPT is far behind at every matched count. pd=4, with 40 times mb724's updates, still trails it (6.74 against 5.18).
  • At pd=3 the gap is in what each step holds. The step count is matched, and so is the average step size, so the gap of 0.48 nats (ln 7.81 − ln 4.83) comes from the steps' contents. It splits about in half:
    • Unequal sizes cost 0.27 nats (4.83 → 6.33, random windows in batches of pd=3's sizes). pd=3's median unit holds 168 events, 2,005 units hold fewer than 24, and the largest holds 212,674, 1.25% of a pass. Under the event-mean rule, Adam gives every one of them a step of about the same size.
    • The trie's grouping costs another 0.21 nats (6.33 → 7.81), against random windows in batches of the same sizes. This is the cost of giving each step only contexts that share their first three characters. The context-tree orientation control found no such cost at pd=1 with a GRU (first-character groups against random groups of the same sizes); at pd=3 with attention the cost is there.
  • Weighting the units by their mass makes it worse. fire_norm: sum scores 8.53 against 7.81 at pd=3. Under Adam, summed gradients give the few big units most of the movement and the thousands of small ones almost none, which cuts the number of updates that count. The size problem calls for units of equal size, not reweighted gradients.
  • Order matters little: shuffled 7.81, radix-id order 8.00.
  • Deeper partitions also cost time. With the exact ancestor gradient, a pd=3 pass takes 53 s and a pd=4 pass 185 s, against 31 s (128 windows) and 45 s (84 windows) for a minibatch pass. So at these depths AGPT is slower per pass as well as worse.
  • Answer to the user's point: the number of updates is necessary. AGPT improves steadily with it, and at pd=1 it is most of the gap. But beyond about 1,500 updates per pass, the composition of each update matters more than how many there are.

Next (not run)

  1. Equal-mass units: a plan whose units hold about the same number of events (1–2k). It would split heavy subtrees deeper and pack light sibling subtrees together. This aims at the size half; mbu3 against mb84 bounds what it can gain (0.27 nats at pd=3). Packing subtrees from different roots into one unit would also attack the grouping half, at the price of some context compute: each packed subtree brings its own ancestors.
  2. The grouping half is the price of prefix sharing. A unit shares computation because its contexts share a prefix, and that same prefix is what correlates its events. Measuring the grouping cost against the compute saved, as the partition depth and packing vary, would map the trade-off AGPT has to win.

Caveats

  • One seed. The seed weights are the AGPT record's; in June they slowed minibatch training compared with random init.
  • AGPT's 65 pd=1 units differ in size (by root character); mbL's 65 steps are equal random batches.
  • Equal passes is not equal compute: training seconds are reported for each run so the comparison can be read either way. They time the trainer process, start-up included: about 1 s for AGPT (loading the trie; a pass is about 6.6 s) and about 2 s for the minibatch trainer (starting PyTorch, reading the corpus; a pass is 28.6 s).
  • Each run's meta.json says git_dirty: true: the trainer and the configs were committed (84054bc) before the runs, but untracked files (this README's summary script, the grid's output) and, for the four lr 0.012 runs, the edited make_configs.py were in the tree.
  • Follow-up runs are dirty for the same reasons: untracked weight files elsewhere in rnd/, the grid's logs, and configs written between commits. The trainer code is the commit each meta.json names: each restart of the grid rebuilt both binaries after a commit, and the one build that came before later edits (18:02:32 UTC, for the runs naming b1019b3) finished a minute before the first of them.
  • Follow-up: one seed. The 2- and 4-pass pd=3 runs tried two rates each, and 0.0015, the higher, won both, so more passes may do somewhat better at a higher rate. pd1s's best one-pass rate is the bottom of its grid.
  • mbu3 matches pd=3's step sizes, not its mix of depths: a window gives one target at each depth 1–16, while a pd=3 unit's targets sit at the depths of its subtree. Rounding to whole windows gives 1,060,418 windows per pass, 801 more than one epoch; the extra ones come from a second random permutation.
